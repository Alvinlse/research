**Background.** The rapid spread of generative AI and scientific computing has intensified competition for GPU resources. Conventional GPU-allocation methods, including reinforcement-learning approaches, primarily optimize aggregate utilization; however, reinforcement-learning policies can be difficult to interpret, diagnose, and improve when operating conditions change. The difficulty is greater for heterogeneous and malleable workloads: jobs arrive irregularly, request different GPU capacities, and may obtain different speedups from additional GPUs. A scheduler must therefore decide both which job to serve and how many GPUs to assign. Increasing one allocation can shorten that job's completion time while consuming disproportionate GPU-hours and delaying other jobs under sublinear speedup, whereas conservative allocation can reduce contention but leave useful parallelism unused. Reliable scheduling consequently requires balancing SLA violations, bounded slowdown, queueing delay, allocation stability, and inter-user fairness. We study this problem using anonymized Slurm records from the MIT Supercloud Dataset [7] and the ElastiSim simulator for rigid and malleable workloads [6].

**Problem Statement.** A fixed scheduling heuristic cannot be assumed to remain effective across states with different queue pressure, arrival intensity, priority composition, and available capacity. The central difficulty is structural: a demand-side objective favors securing enough GPUs to reduce a job's waiting time, whereas a supply-side objective favors preserving capacity and distributing resources across competing jobs. A single LLM given all information may produce one globally plausible recommendation without explicitly representing this conflict. Directly allowing an LLM to emit job-level allocations also introduces infeasible decisions, unstable behavior, and non-negligible inference cost. We therefore formulate the research question as follows: **Can demand–supply multi-LLM negotiation outperform a single LLM at a matched inference budget?** More specifically, given only information available to an online scheduler, can a demand reviewer and a supply reviewer provide statements that enable a referee to select a policy with lower regret than a single-LLM selector, while deterministic code preserves feasibility? The selector must not observe true job runtimes, must generalize to unseen workload windows, and must be compared with strong fixed-policy and zero-shot baselines.

**Related Work.** Traditional HPC scheduling uses interpretable rules such as first-come, first-served and EASY backfilling [1]. Learning-based schedulers formulate resource allocation as a sequential decision problem: DeepRM applies deep reinforcement learning to cluster resource management [2], while Decima learns scheduling and parallelism policies for directed-acyclic-graph workloads [3]. Subsequent work has investigated distilling neural scheduling policies into interpretable decision trees [4] and applying LLMs to cost-aware task scheduling in cloud systems [5]. In parallel, multi-agent debate and self-consistency have been proposed to improve language-model reasoning, although their gains are often confounded with additional inference calls. Our experiments isolate this confound with matched-budget controls. Rather than allowing a learned model to allocate GPUs directly, our approach uses counterfactual simulation as a privileged teacher and restricts the online model to selecting among deterministic, independently validated policies.

**Proposed Method.** We implement a Policy-Selector Referee with three logically separated roles. The demand-side LLM receives queue-oriented evidence—waiting demand, waiting-time and laxity summaries, requested sizes, wall-time information, production jobs, and malleability—and emits a structured statement recommending one policy. The supply-side LLM receives capacity-oriented evidence—free and running capacity, recent arrival load, running-job ages, production occupancy, and the current policy—and emits an independent statement. A referee LLM then receives the complete observable state, the policy menu, and both statements; it selects one ordering rule and one sizing rule, but never names a job or a GPU count. The offline action library contains 15 ordering–sizing combinations: market-based ordering, least-laxity ordering, fairness-based ordering, production-tier and requested-wall-time ordering, and conservative admission without resizing, each crossed with requested-size allocation, adaptive sharing, and greedy expansion. The structurally unsafe combination of conservative non-resizing admission with greedy expansion remains an auditable offline negative control but is rejected online. Deterministic scheduler code executes the selected pair and preserves the previous safe policy whenever the referee output is invalid or unsafe. We evaluate this opposed demand–supply arm against a one-call selector, a three-sample single-prompt control, and a symmetric three-call reviewer so that role specialization is separated from inference budget.

**Methodology.** We generate supervision with counterfactual ElastiSim rollouts. For each workload window, a market-based ordering with requested-size allocation is run from the beginning, and up to 12 states are sampled between 5% and 60% of the interval. Each candidate policy is then evaluated from the same sampled state. The initial corpus used a remainder-of-window target; because the online selector acts for the next interval, we subsequently implemented a fixed 1,800-second decision-horizon teacher that returns every candidate to a common continuation policy. Rewards combine SLA-10 violations, bounded slowdown, mean waiting time, resizing frequency, and Jain-style user fairness; true runtime is available only to the offline teacher and is excluded from all referee packets. The leakage-corrected corpus contains 589 states from 54 workload windows, split by complete window into 407 training, 88 validation, and 94 test states. Policies whose reward is within 0.05 of the best policy are retained as equivalent alternatives, and the unsafe combination is defined from mechanism semantics rather than held-out outcomes. We report regret over the permitted policy set and the full candidate set, unsafe-selection rate, parsing failures, number of model calls, and latency against zero-shot, random, majority, best-fixed, tree, and ridge baselines. The corrected offline results currently show no learned-selector improvement over the best fixed policy, and the low-rank adaptation retraining and multi-window closed-loop comparison remain pending; accordingly, all present findings are preliminary.
