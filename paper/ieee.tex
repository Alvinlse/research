Evidence from Elastic GPU Cluster Scheduling}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Lay Kim Seng}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Cyberscience Center, Tohoku University\\
lay.kim.seng.q4@dc.tohoku.ac.jp}

\begin{abstract}
A GPU job that can be resized makes two demands on a scheduler: which job to start, and how many
devices to give it. Trace-driven studies almost always erase the second decision by replaying each
job at its recorded size, and we show that this choice decides the outcome of the study. Replaying
the MIT Supercloud accounting log rigidly on an $80$-GPU pool, five standard schedulers differ by at
most $1.3$ percentage points of occupancy, recover on average $0.2\%$ of idle capacity, and separate
on no service metric after correction: the comparison is close to vacuous by construction. Restoring
elasticity through a malleability-aware simulator makes the same metric controllable and reverses its
meaning. Sizing then moves service quality by an order of magnitude more than any ordering rule, and
the sizing rule that attains the highest occupancy we measure, $0.988$ against $0.890$, is
simultaneously the worst on every service axis, because sublinear speed-up converts capacity into
busy-ness rather than into completed work. On twelve held-out windows the four queue-ordering rules we test span
under one percentage point of deadline violations while sizing spans $15.1$ to $47.1$, and among
auction valuations only pricing a job's deadline pressure matters, $8.1$ against $14.5$ to $15.0$.
The advantage of auction pricing over queue ordering is concentrated in one overloaded window of
twelve, where violations fall from $91.0\%$ to $21.6\%$ while the median difference is zero.
Counterfactual enumeration of every policy from sampled states bounds what any state-dependent
selector could add at $2.03$ points for queue ordering and $0.07$ for auctions, with a median of zero.
Asked to select policies from that same observable state, a language model does not approach the
bound: on held-out states an 8B model carries $8.70$ points of mean regret against the deterministic
policy's $1.05$, and emits an illegal action on 11 of 24 states where deterministic code emits none.
Low-rank fine-tuning changes nothing, at $8.78$ points and the same 11 failures. Splitting the
referee into demand and supply analysts does not help either: it costs $0.67$ points against a single
referee for three times the calls, on twelve paired windows of which seven tie exactly. We conclude
that numeric rationing on this workload belongs to deterministic code, and that the case for a
language model in a scheduler has to be made where the deciding information is not numeric at all.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

AI training and inference are the dominant consumers of GPU clusters, and they are
\emph{elastic}: a training job can run on more or fewer devices, trading device-hours against
completion time. A scheduler on such a workload therefore makes two decisions per job, not one
--- \emph{which} job to start, and \emph{how large} to make it.

Almost every published evaluation erases the second decision. A trace records that a job used
$g$ devices for $t$ seconds; it does not record what that job would have done on $2g$ or on
$g/2$, because the counterfactual was never observed. The standard response is to replay the
trace \emph{rigidly}, pinning each job to its historical size. This is usually presented as a
conservative simplification. We show it is a suppression, and that it silently invalidates the
efficiency claims most commonly made on top of it.

The argument is short. If no job can be resized, the device-seconds a workload consumes are
fixed: every scheduler must run the same jobs for the same durations, so it can only move work
earlier or later. Utilisation is then close to a conserved quantity, and a comparison of
schedulers on it is close to a comparison of nothing. Restoring elasticity does not merely add
a tuning knob; it makes utilisation controllable, and --- as we show --- makes maximising it
actively harmful.

Our contributions, all on the MIT Supercloud GPU trace~\cite{supercloud} replayed through a malleability-aware
batch simulator~\cite{elastisim}, are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Rigid replay cannot answer an efficiency question} (\S\ref{sec:rigid}). We quantify
how little room a fixed-size replay leaves: occupancy differs by at most $1.3$ points across five
standard schedulers, recoverable idle capacity averages $0.2\%$, and no arm separates on service
quality. Backfilling is inert on this trace because requested walltimes exceed true runtimes by two
to three orders of magnitude.
\item \textbf{Occupancy is an anti-metric once jobs are elastic} (\S\ref{sec:rigid}). Sizing moves service quality by an order of magnitude more than ordering
does, and the highest-occupancy rule is the worst-service rule in every window we measure. We
recommend reporting device-hours per unit of completed work instead.
\item \textbf{Language-model policy selection does not match deterministic code} (\S\ref{sec:controls},
\S\ref{sec:oracle}). On held-out windows the ordering rule is worth under one point while
sizing is worth a factor of three and the auction's pricing rule six points, and the advantage of
auction pricing over ordering is a single-window tail effect.
Counterfactual enumeration bounds the available headroom at $2.03$ and $0.07$ points with a median of
zero, and neither a zero-shot model, a low-rank fine-tune, nor a demand--supply role split comes near
it: eight times the deterministic policy's regret, and an illegal action on 11 of 24 held-out states.
\item \textbf{Role separation does not pay for itself} (\S\ref{sec:factorial-results}). Splitting the
referee into demand and supply analysts costs $0.67$ deadline points against a single referee for three
times the inference, and is the only condition that emitted invalid rulings.
\item \textbf{A protocol that makes such a comparison auditable} (\S\ref{sec:factorial-method}). The
selector study is frozen before execution: workload-only window selection, a hashed manifest of
trace, worlds, code and configuration, a fail-closed gate, and amendments recorded rather than
absorbed.
\end{enumerate}

A negative result of this kind is only worth reporting if the comparison was fair, so we spend
\S\ref{sec:factorial-method} on the protocol: the windows are chosen from workload statistics alone,
the deterministic baseline is selected on validation and never on the reserved split, the action space
and the model configuration are identical across structures, and a budget-matched control separates the
effect of role separation from the effect of spending three times the calls.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{LLM-driven scheduling.} LLMsched~\cite{llmsched} repairs a single LLM's proposal with
an ILP and reports large gains over Kubernetes. We inherit the reason-then-guarantee spine but
invert its default: on numeric rationing the deterministic layer should be the proposer, not
just the repairer, and the LLM should be gated behind a contextual trigger.
% TODO before submission: 2--3 more LLM-scheduling references

\textbf{Learning-based schedulers.} DRL schedulers (DeepRM~\cite{deeprm},
Decima~\cite{decima}) learn allocation policies but are black-box and brittle under
distribution shift. On our interface a tabular Q-learner's episode-return variance dominates
the action effect at realisable sample counts.

\textbf{Multi-agent LLM structures.} Multiagent debate~\cite{du2024debate} and
self-consistency~\cite{wang2023selfconsistency} are usually evaluated on QA benchmarks, and
LLM-as-judge protocols~\cite{zheng2023judge} inherit that setting; budget-matched controls are
rare, so a reported gain is rarely separable from the extra calls that buy it. Every structure
comparison we report carries such a control.

\textbf{Malleability and mechanism design.} Runtime elasticity is rarely deployed in
production~\cite{tarraf2024}; our partial-allocation model rests on it and we state that
dependence. Uniform-price auctions are the classical route to truthful revelation, and
automated negotiation for resource allocation is a classical subject~\cite{boan2011}, with
prediction-guided \emph{selective} negotiation explored in one-shot fair
division~\cite{croitoru2025}; our contribution is measurement in a closed loop containing
declarative LLM agents.

\section{Controlled Policy-Selector Architecture}
\label{sec:arch}


Both reasoning structures read the same state and emit the same action.  At each 1,800-second selection
point the model sees available capacity and scheduler-visible attributes of pending and running
jobs: arrival and waiting time, requested walltime and GPU count, priority, deadline pressure,
legal malleable bounds, and scaling indicators.  It never sees true runtime or future arrivals.

\textbf{Single Referee.} One model returns strict JSON containing one global ordering policy, a
default sizing rule, and optional job-specific sizing overrides.  The parser expands this into
exactly one action for every affected job.

\textbf{Demand--Supply--Referee.} Demand identifies urgent, deadline-pressured, scalable, and
shrink-tolerant jobs. Supply describes scarcity, queue pressure, resize cost, and fairness.  These
agents cannot allocate GPUs; their analyses and the original state are passed to a Referee whose
output schema is identical to the Single arm.

\textbf{The action set.} Both structures choose from the same two-part action, and the two policy
families are matched in size so that neither referee simply has more options than the other.

The \emph{queue-ordering} family decides which waiting job is considered first: first-come
first-served, least-laxity-first on the requested walltime, ageing fair-share on accumulated
per-user waiting, and priority-tier ordering that clears the high-priority tier first. The
\emph{auction} family replaces that comparison with a single uniform-price clearing and varies only
what a job's claim is priced on: the time it has already waited, its deadline pressure, its owner's
waiting-service deficit, or its priority tier. Holding the clearing rule fixed isolates the
valuation, which is the design decision an operator actually makes.

Crossed with either family, four sizing rules decide how many devices a started job receives, and
govern later resize points as well. \emph{Requested size} gives a job exactly what its owner asked
for and makes it wait until that much is free; this reproduces the rigid replay and is the control.
\emph{Queue-proportional} sizing shares the free pool across the waiting queue, so a job may start
smaller when others are waiting and grow when they are not. \emph{Maximum-fit} sizing gives every
job the largest legal size that fits at the moment it starts. \emph{Benefit-gated} sizing changes a
running job's size only when the estimated gain, measured in seconds against the longest current
wait and charged for the extra runtime that shrinking causes, exceeds a fixed threshold of $300$\,s;
its estimates use requested walltimes only, never true runtimes.

A shared validator rejects malformed actions, enforces the $80$-GPU capacity and each job's legal
bounds, and invokes a counted deterministic fallback, so the comparison changes only the policy
family and the reasoning structure.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\textbf{Setup.} We replay the MIT Supercloud GPU trace~\cite{supercloud} (a $235$-day Slurm accounting log)
through ElastiSim~\cite{elastisim}, a batch-system simulator for malleable workloads. One
simulated node is one GPU, so a job that used $g$ GPUs becomes a $g$-node job; its work is set
so that it reproduces its recorded runtime exactly. The scheduler sees each job's arrival, size
and \emph{requested} walltime, never its true runtime --- the same information a production
batch system has. Capacity is a fixed $80$-GPU pool, so a window's offered load is a property
we \emph{measure} rather than a consequence of sizing the cluster to fit it.

\textbf{Window selection is pre-registered.} Every $24$\,h window of the trace is described by
workload statistics alone --- job count, GPU-hours, offered load, high-priority fraction,
median and p90 runtime, size distribution --- with no scheduler executed. Of $5{,}613$ hourly
starts, $4{,}312$ carry at least $100$ jobs. These are stratified into four offered-load bands
($0.7$--$1.0$, $1.0$--$1.5$, $1.5$--$2.5$, ${>}2.5\times$) crossed with three high-priority
bands (${<}2\%$, $2$--$10\%$, ${>}10\%$), and one window is drawn per cell under a fixed seed,
subject to a non-overlap constraint. The resulting $12$ windows span days $41$--$222$, offered
loads $0.75$--$4.97\times$ and priority fractions $0.7$--$58\%$. Each is preceded by $12$\,h of
warm-up arrivals, which the arm under test also schedules but which are excluded from scoring,
so no run begins on an empty cluster. Selection never sees a scheduler result.

\textbf{Elasticity model.} A job may be \emph{moldable}: its size is chosen once, at launch, and
fixed thereafter. Speed-up follows Amdahl's law anchored on the observed point --- a job seen at
$g$ GPUs reproduces its recorded runtime exactly at $g$, so the rigid replay is this same model
evaluated at the size the job really ran, and the parallel fraction $s$ is swept rather than
assumed ($s{=}1$ is rigid at every size, $s{=}0$ is linear speed-up). Results below use
$s{=}0.7$, a deliberately conservative value: scaling is sublinear, so growing a job costs more
device-time than it saves. We report SLA-$k$ (turnaround exceeding $k\times$ true runtime) at
$k \in \{2,5,10\}$, mean waiting time, device-hours actually spent, walltime kills, and
occupancy over the measured window.

\subsection{Frozen factorial protocol}
\label{sec:factorial-method}

The core experiment uses 48 non-overlapping 24-hour windows, each with 12 hours of warm-up,
selected from workload characteristics before scheduling outcomes are inspected.  They are frozen
as 24 train, 12 validation, and 12 test windows.  Every scored window contains at least 100 jobs.
Jobs are malleable at ten resize points with bounds
$1\leq g_i(t)\leq\min(80,4g_i^{\mathrm{trace}})$, $s=0.7$, and no modeled resize overhead.
Synthetic deadlines are generated once as
$D_i=a_i+W_i^{\mathrm{req}}$; a missing walltime receives the fixed 86,400-second estimate.
Deadline violation rate is the primary outcome.

The four inferential conditions cross the two reasoning structures with the two policy families.
Qwen2.5-14B is held fixed at temperature 0.1 and a 400-token output limit, and the two structures see
an identical state, action set, parser, and validator, so the only difference between them is that the
role-separated arm spends three calls on two analyses and a ruling where the single referee spends one.
Thirty-two deterministic controls exhaustively cross the four ordering or pricing rules with the four
sizing rules in each family.  Validation selects the best fixed policy per family; the reserved split
is opened once.  The inferential unit is the window.  The preregistered analysis contains exactly three
two-sided paired contrasts on the reserved split: the family main effect, the structure main effect,
and their difference-in-differences interaction, with paired-$t$ 95\% confidence intervals, exact
sign-flip tests, and Holm correction across those three.

Role separation spends three calls where the single referee spends one, so the design also carries a
\emph{budget-matched control}: the single prompt sampled three times with a majority vote.  It matches
call count and output budget but necessarily not temperature, $0.8$ against $0.1$, since a vote over
three samples at $0.1$ returns near-identical answers and collapses into the single-call arm.  It was
added after a pilot on the four highest-load \emph{training} windows, run to decide whether the
remaining inference was worth its cost; training windows were used so that no pilot observation touches
the reserved split, and no pilot number is reported here.  Two further amendments are recorded with the
protocol rather than absorbed: a correction to the feasibility assertion, which had excluded completed
but not killed jobs from ownership accounting and aborted valid runs without affecting any policy or
metric, and a reduction from three inference repetitions to one, since at $1{,}703$\,s per
role-separated window against $251$\,s for the single referee the three-repetition design needed a
further $64$ hours.  That reduction merges decoding noise into the window-level differences, which makes
the paired tests more conservative rather than less.

Every result row stores hashes of the trace, the generated world, the implementation, the manifest and
the revision, and a fail-closed gate re-checks all of them, with the split, window non-overlap, per-job
deadlines, malleable bounds and speed-up anchoring, before any run executes.  Superseded hashes are
retained, so rows produced before an amendment stay verifiable instead of being restamped.  Runtime
assertions enforce capacity, malleable bounds, non-negative remaining work and single completion at
every scheduler invocation.

\subsection{Why elasticity, and why not occupancy}
\label{sec:rigid}

Two properties of this workload shape everything that follows, and both are measured rather than
assumed.

\textbf{A rigid replay has almost nothing to compare.} With every job pinned to its historical size,
the device-seconds consumed are fixed by the window, so a scheduler can only move work earlier or
later.  Across five schedulers --- strict first-come first-served, first-fit, EASY
backfilling~\cite{easy}, requested-walltime ordering, and a declared/undeclared split --- occupancy
differs by at most $1.3$ points within a window and is identical to three decimals in several, the
idle capacity any ordering rule could still recover averages $0.2\%$, and over $11$ windows no arm
separates from the backfilling baseline after Holm correction across three slowdown thresholds (largest
raw effect $-4.2$ points, $p_{\text{Holm}}=0.41$).  Two trace properties explain the flatness and are
usually assumed away.  Backfilling is inert here: across $1{,}351$ blocked scheduling points it
backfilled $48$ times, because requested walltimes exceed true runtimes by $100$--$600\times$, so no
job can be shown to fit before the head job's reservation.  And requested walltime carries no runtime
signal: its rank correlation with true runtime across four windows is $+0.16$, $-0.11$, $-0.73$ and
$-0.01$, the only significant value being negative.  Any shortest-job-first rule on this trace orders
on noise.

\textbf{Once jobs are elastic, occupancy inverts.}  Holding ordering at first-fit and varying only
sizing over the same $12$ windows, requested-size sizing spends $3{,}653$ device-hours at $0.890$
occupancy for $22.3\%$ slowdown violations; queue-proportional spends $3{,}339$ at $0.964$ for
$32.5\%$; maximum-fit spends $9{,}581$ at $0.988$ for $76.2\%$.  Paired against requested size,
maximum-fit costs $+5{,}929 \pm 1{,}707$ device-hours, $+232{,}451 \pm 60{,}823$\,s of waiting and
$+53.9 \pm 12.3$ points of violations, an order of magnitude beyond anything ordering moved.  The rule
with the \emph{highest} occupancy is thus the worst on every service axis, in $12$ of $12$ windows,
because under sublinear speed-up a job given $4\times$ the devices at $s{=}0.7$ finishes about
$1.6\times$ sooner while consuming $4\times$ the capacity.  Occupancy records the resulting busy-ness
and is blind to the waste: on one low-load window it reads $1.000$ for all three sizing rules while
their mean waiting spans $21{,}429$ to $244{,}781$\,s.  We therefore report device-hours per unit of
completed work alongside waiting time, and treat occupancy as diagnostic rather than as an objective.

The obvious fix for over-sizing does not work either.  Queue-proportional sizing does not beat
requested size on waiting ($+1{,}684 \pm 10{,}847$\,s, $p{=}0.77$) or device-hours ($-313 \pm 637$,
$p{=}0.36$) and is significantly worse on violations ($+10.2 \pm 8.3$ points, $p{=}0.035$), because
shrinking a job lengthens it and can push it past its requested limit.  What remains open is sizing
under genuine runtime uncertainty, on a trace whose only estimate is uninformative, and that is the
decision we hand to the selectors below.

Paired over the twelve windows of the ordering family, separating the referee into demand and supply

One measurement trap is worth stating because it is easy to repeat and we fell into it first.
Averaging each condition over whatever repetitions had finished, thirty-six runs for the single-referee
conditions against twenty-one for the role-separated one, placed role separation $0.81$ points
\emph{ahead}.  Restricting to the one repetition every window shares reverses the sign.  The same
hazard applies to the deterministic controls: the best queue-ordering configuration reads $8.08\%$ on
the nine test windows that finished first and $15.27\%$ once the eleventh arrives, because windows

auction family, each by validation violations alone with a deterministic tie-break, and each carried
unchanged to the reserved split.  We do not draw a selector-against-fixed-policy comparison here:
assembling it from one completed split is exactly the unbalanced reading described above, and
\S\ref{sec:future} sets out what would settle it.

\subsection{Inside the action space: sizing and pricing dominate ordering}
\label{sec:controls}

The 32 deterministic controls are complete on both splits: every configuration ran on all 12 validation
and all 12 test windows.  They characterise the action space the selectors choose within, independently
of any selector.  All figures below are paired over the 12 held-out windows.


Two facts constrain what any selector could contribute.  First, the ordering rule is the least
consequential part of the action: the four orderings span $14.48$ to $15.44$ points, under one point,
while sizing under a fixed ordering spans $15.07$ to $47.06$, a factor of three.  Ordering is not
literally inert, but it is worth roughly a thirtieth of what sizing is worth, which reproduces
\S\ref{sec:rigid} inside the larger $8\times4$ space, on held-out windows, and with deadline
violations rather than slowdown as the outcome.  Second, within the auction family the clearing
mechanism is held fixed and only what a claim is \emph{priced on} moves the outcome: pricing deadline
pressure gives $8.11$ points against $14.48$--$15.03$ for wait, priority, and fair-share pricing.  The
decisions that matter are therefore how much capacity each job receives and what its claim is priced
on, not the order in which the queue is scanned.

The difference between the two families is a tail effect and has to be reported as one.  The best
auction configuration beats the best ordering configuration by $6.38$ points on average, while the
median difference across the 12 windows is exactly zero: seven windows tie to the digit, four move by
$0.5$ to $3.8$ points, and one moves by $69.4$, from $91.0\%$ violations to $21.6\%$.  That window is
the only one in the set where the cluster is overwhelmed, and it is where almost the entire mean
difference lives.  A mean-only reading would present a one-window rescue as a general property of
auction clearing.

One note on how this table reached completeness, since it bears on the value of running the checks at
all.  Six configurations on one test window initially aborted, the capacity assertion reporting 81
devices allocated from a pool of 80.  The cause was in the checker, not the scheduler: the simulator
retains a killed job's assignment in its mirror for the job's callback while already offering those
devices as free, and the assertion counted the stale record.  Completed jobs were already excluded and
killed ones now are.  Re-running the six configurations after that correction produced no violation,
and no allocation in this paper ever exceeded the pool.  The episode is worth a sentence because a
capacity assertion that fires on a bookkeeping artifact is indistinguishable, from the outside, from
one that catches a real over-allocation, and only reproducing it separates the two.

\subsection{Counterfactual oracle and LoRA protocol}
\label{sec:oracle}

To test whether supervision rather than architecture is the bottleneck, we clone sampled simulator
states and execute all 16 policy--sizing actions in the relevant family from the same state, with
the same future arrivals and seed.  The lowest deadline-violation action supplies an offline label;
future outcomes are never included in the online prompt.  We sample two states per window and
family.  Four states from one training window were discarded under the superseded capacity assertion described
in \S\ref{sec:controls}; they are recoverable and their inclusion would slightly change the bound below.  The resulting 1,092 auditable rollouts
form 44 complete training examples and 24 validation examples, with disjoint windows and no
oracle-only fields in model input.


Enumerating from sampled states, the best action per state improves on the fixed policy by $2.31$
points on training and $2.03$ on validation for the ordering family, and by $0.51$ and $0.07$ for the
auction family.  The median gain is zero in every one of those four cells: selection matters in a
minority of states, especially in the non-market family, while a strong fixed market policy leaves
almost no mean headroom.  We fine-tune Qwen3-8B with rank-16 LoRA on assistant spans only and judge
it on the 24 held-out states by valid-action rate, oracle-action agreement, mean counterfactual
regret, and improvement over the best fixed training action.  This evaluation is deliberately
offline: it determines whether the model learned the selector before another expensive closed-loop
sweep is warranted.

It did not.  On the 24 held-out states, the deterministic policy carries $1.05$ points of mean regret
against the counterfactual best action, a zero median, $75.0\%$ of states within $0.1$ points of that
action, and no invalid actions.  Zero-shot, the model carries $8.70$ points, a $0.8$ median, $45.8\%$
near-optimal, and 11 of 24 illegal or unparseable actions.  Fine-tuned, it carries $8.78$ points, the
same median, $41.7\%$ near-optimal, and the same 11 failures.


Neither language-model arm is competitive.  Both carry roughly eight times the deterministic policy's
regret, both leave a median state $0.8$ points short of the best available action where the
deterministic policy leaves it exactly at the optimum, and both emit an unparseable or illegal action
on 11 of 24 states, which the deterministic policy never does.  Fine-tuning moves nothing: mean regret
is marginally worse than zero-shot, the median is identical, near-optimal states fall, and the invalid
count is unchanged.  An initial reasoning-enabled decode was worse still, at 23 of 24 invalid, so both
reported arms use the non-thinking template for this strict-JSON task; invalid outputs are
conservatively scored as the worst candidate action.

The closed-loop numbers agree with the offline screening.  On the validation split the single referee
reaches $11.27\%$ deadline violations on the ordering family and $10.70\%$ on the auction family, and
the role-separated referee $11.93\%$ on the ordering family, against $10.49\%$ and $9.87\%$ for the
deterministic policies selected on that same split.  Those fixed policies were chosen on these
windows, so this is not a held-out comparison and we do not test it; the held-out evidence is
the held-out regret above, and it points the same way.  Taken together, a small language model
asked to choose scheduling policies from observable state --- zero-shot or fine-tuned, alone or split
into demand and supply roles --- does not match a single well-chosen deterministic policy on this
workload, and a closed-loop fine-tuned sweep is not warranted by this screening.

This is a statement about the numeric rationing decision, not about language models in schedulers.
The same deterministic policy that dominates here is helpless when the deciding fact is not in the
numbers at all, which is the case we turn to next.

\section{Future Work and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:future}
\label{sec:threats}

\textbf{The reserved split is unfinished.}  Three of the four selector conditions are complete on
validation and none on the reserved split, so every selector number here is a validation observation
rather than the preregistered confirmatory analysis, and we claim no family, structure, or interaction
effect.  Completing it is the immediate next step, together with two analyses the existing labels
already support: per-state regret against the counterfactual best action, which separates a selector
that picks a harmless equivalent from one that picks a damaging action, and a split on a contention
condition defined from observable state alone, which is where the one-window effect of
\S\ref{sec:controls} lives.  Reducing three repetitions to one also merges decoding noise into the
window-level differences and widens any interval we would report.

\textbf{The elasticity model is an assumption, and a swept one.} No trace records what a job would
have done at a different size, so any elastic study must supply that counterfactual.  We use Amdahl's
law anchored on the observed point, which reproduces each job's recorded runtime exactly at the size it
really used, and we sweep the parallel fraction rather than fixing it: $s{=}1$ recovers the rigid
replay at every size, $s{=}0$ gives linear speed-up.  Our conclusions concern the shape of the result
across that sweep.  Reconfiguration cost is not modelled, which flatters resize-heavy policies and
makes our claim against occupancy-maximising sizing conservative; we report resize counts with zero
modelled overhead explicitly.  One node is modelled as one device, so intra-node placement effects are
absent.

\textbf{The outcome metrics are synthetic and imperfect.}  The trace carries no deadline field, so
deadlines are generated once from requested walltime and the primary result concerns that reproducible
synthetic target, not production deadline compliance.  Slowdown-based violations are defined from true
runtime, which no scheduler knows at submission, and their allowance scales with job length, so we
report three thresholds and lead with waiting time and device-hours.

\textbf{The model evidence is narrow.}  The offline comparison rests on 24 held-out states and a single
fine-tuning run, and the model there is an 8B model while the closed loop uses a 14B one, so the
fine-tuning failure is screening evidence against this recipe rather than proof that supervision cannot
work.  Counterfactual enumeration bounds what a selector could achieve under this simulator; it is not
evidence that an online model could identify those actions.  Four training states dropped under the
superseded capacity assertion remain to be relabelled.

\section{Conclusion}

How a trace-driven study handles job size decides what it can find.  Replayed rigidly, this workload
leaves standard schedulers separated by at most $1.3$ points of occupancy and by nothing on service
quality, so efficiency comparisons drawn on it compare a conserved quantity.  Restoring elasticity makes
the metric controllable and then maximising it becomes the wrong objective, because the rule that fills
the cluster most completely is the worst on every service axis.  For elastic workloads, device-hours per
unit of completed work belongs in the headline and occupancy does not.

Within that frame our title question has a clear answer for numeric rationing: no.  Counterfactual
enumeration bounds what any state-dependent selector could add at $2.03$ points for ordering and $0.07$
for auctions, with zero median gain.  Asked to choose in that space, an 8B model carries $8.70$ points
of held-out regret zero-shot and $8.78$ after fine-tuning, against $1.05$ for one deterministic policy,
and emits an illegal action on eleven of twenty-four states where the deterministic policy emits none.
Splitting the referee into demand and supply analysts costs $0.67$ points for three times the inference.
Neither structure nor supervision closes a gap this wide, because the contested decision is arithmetic
the code already performs correctly.

Where a language model might still earn its place is the case this workload cannot express: an exception
whose content is text, such as an operator instruction or a declaration that a note contradicts.  The
accounting log carries 29 fields and none is free text, and no public GPU trace we surveyed carries
operator-authored rationale, so that case cannot be measured here at all.  We flag it as the open
direction.  The recommendation follows the evidence rather than the architecture: keep a validated
deterministic default, count every malformed answer and fallback, and admit a language model only where a
held-out regret test and a paired closed-loop comparison beat that default after inference cost.
